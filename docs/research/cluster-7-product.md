# Research — Cluster VII: The product as experienced and valued (Product, Experience & Value)

> Part of code-quality-atlas phase-1 research (see ../taxonomy.md). Written 2026-08-07 from the main loop under the behavioral-claims rule in [README.md](README.md). Citations verified except where marked `(verify)`. Mirrors the section structure of [cluster-1-correctness.md](cluster-1-correctness.md).
>
> **Cluster scope.** Six clusters cover the code and its lifecycle; none covered the product **as experienced and valued by the people who use it** ([map-gaps G24](../map-gaps.md#g24--candidate-cluster-vii-product-experience--value)). This file opens that cluster with its two highest-leverage members, per G24's own incremental disposition (VII-A + VII-F first). VII-C (design-system conformance), VII-D (UX writing), VII-G (transparency), VII-H (conceptual integrity), and VII-J (feature value lifecycle) remain unbuilt candidates; VII-E and VII-I fold toward #23.
>
> **The cluster's standing constraints, inherited by every member.** (1) **Skip when there is no user-facing surface** — a backend service, CLI, library, or infra repo is out of scope, exactly as #23 and #25 already skip. (2) **Detect-and-route (G23)**: surfacing is not deciding. These lenses name what they see with evidence and route the *decision* to design or product; they never block an engineering merge on a taste call. (3) But routing is not softening: a missing error state and a destructive action with no way back are **defects** with ordinary engineering severity, not preferences — the routed half is which outcome to chase and which pattern to prefer, not whether the code handles the states it can produce.

## #42 Usability & interaction quality

> **Promoted v0.12 (map-gaps G24, VII-A); lens built this phase.** The first member of Cluster VII, and the first lens in the suite whose subject is the *user's* experience of a change rather than the code's properties. It asks the question a heuristic evaluation asks: given this flow, can a person tell what is happening, do what they came to do, and recover when it goes wrong?
>
> **G1 single-owner & boundary.** #42 owns **interaction quality** — system-status feedback, reversibility of destructive actions, error recovery, the completeness of the states a screen can be in, and conformity with the expectations the product has already set. It does **not** own accessibility mechanics (**#23** — keyboard, ARIA, contrast, screen-reader semantics; a flow can be perfectly accessible and still baffling, and vice versa), *measured* performance (**#15** — INP, TTFB, bundle size; #42 owns whether the wait is *communicated*, #15 whether it exists), manipulative design (**#36** — a dark pattern is a deliberate harm; #42's findings are ordinary usability failures with no intent behind them), code-level consistency (**#8** — #42's consistency is the one a *user* perceives), or adopter-facing setup and upgrade experience (**#33** — a different audience entirely).
>
> **Folded in deliberately: VII-B's state completeness.** "Does every state this code can produce have a designed screen" was catalogued separately as VII-B (experiential quality). It is folded here rather than promoted, because it is the single most diff-visible interaction defect and has no other owner — an async call introduces loading, empty, and error states whether or not anyone designs them. The rest of VII-B (perceived-performance technique, optimistic UI, transition polish) stays unbuilt.
>
> **Detect-and-route, calibrated.** Two dispositions, and the split is what keeps this lens from becoming a taste tribunal. **Defect** (ordinary severity, engineering owns the fix): a state the code reaches with nothing rendered for it, a destructive action with neither undo nor a confirmation that names what is lost, an error that discards the user's input, an operation that reports nothing on completion. **`route: design` / `route: product`** (surfaced with evidence, sets no engineering verdict): which pattern is better, whether the flow should exist, wording, visual treatment, and any judgment that rests on knowing the users.

### Key references

- **Jakob Nielsen — "10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design" (NN/g, 1994; revised since)** — <https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ten-usability-heuristics/> → mine: the canonical completeness model for this category, and the reason the lens can claim coverage at all. Four of the ten are directly diff-visible and become the lens's spine — *visibility of system status*, *user control and freedom* (the "emergency exit"), *error prevention*, and *help users recognize, diagnose, and recover from errors*. The remaining six (match with the real world, consistency and standards, recognition over recall, flexibility and efficiency, aesthetic and minimalist design, help and documentation) are reviewable but lean judgment-side, so they route rather than block.
- **Jakob Nielsen — "Severity Ratings for Usability Problems" (NN/g)** → mine: usability findings carry their own severity scale (frequency × impact × persistence), which is *not* the atlas's Blocker/Major/Minor/Nit. Rather than import a second scale, map: a state with no rendering or an unrecoverable destructive action is Major on the atlas scale because it is a functional gap; everything that needs a designer's judgment routes without a severity. URL `(verify)`.
- **Don Norman — *The Design of Everyday Things*** → mine: two ideas the lens uses directly. The **gulf of evaluation** (can the user tell what state the system is in?) is the reviewable form of "visibility of system status" — a mutation that fires with no feedback leaves the user unable to evaluate anything. And **slips vs. mistakes**: a slip is the right intention executed wrongly, which is why a destructive control that shares size, colour, and position with its safe neighbour is a design defect rather than user error.
- **Jakob Nielsen — response-time limits (*Usability Engineering*, 1993)** → mine: ~0.1s reads as instantaneous, ~1s keeps the user's flow of thought unbroken, ~10s is the limit of attention for a task. The reviewable consequence is not the number but the obligation: past the first threshold an operation must *say* it is working, and past the last it must let the user leave and come back. #15 owns making it fast; #42 owns what the interface does while it is not.
- **ISO/IEC 25010:2023 — the *interaction capability* characteristic** — <https://quality.arc42.org/articles/iso-25010-update-2023> → mine: the 2023 revision renamed *usability* to **interaction capability** and separated its sub-characteristics, among them *appropriateness recognizability*, *learnability*, *operability*, *user error protection*, *user engagement*, *inclusivity*, and *self-descriptiveness*. Two things follow: **user error protection** is a named, standardised quality (not a nicety), and **inclusivity** is where G24's VII-E belongs when it is built — as an extension of #23, not here.
- **ISO 9241-110:2020 — *Ergonomics of human-system interaction, Part 110: Interaction principles*** → mine: the standards-track counterpart to Nielsen's heuristics, revising the older "dialogue principles". Its principles include *suitability for the user's tasks*, *self-descriptiveness*, *conformity with user expectations*, *learnability*, *controllability*, and *use error robustness* `(verify — enumeration and exact titles against the standard; the specific count is deliberately not asserted here)`. Mined for one idea Nielsen states less sharply: **controllability** — the user, not the system, sets the pace and direction of the interaction. That is what a modal with no dismiss, a wizard with no back, and an auto-advancing step each violate.
- **Jakob's Law (NN/g)** — <https://www.nngroup.com/articles/end-of-web-design/> → mine: users spend most of their time in *other* products, so they arrive expecting your product to work like those. The reviewable form is narrow and checkable: a new control that does a job the product already does elsewhere should behave like the existing one, and a deviation should have a reason recorded rather than being noticed in review. `(verify — URL)`
- **#36 `reviewing-ethical-design` — the sibling, and the boundary that matters most.** Both lenses read user-facing flows and both detect-and-route. The difference is intent and harm: #36 owns designs that disadvantage the user *to someone else's benefit* (sneaking, confirmshaming, obstruction, manipulative defaults); #42 owns designs that fail the user with nobody benefiting. Obstruction is the pair's genuine edge case — deliberate friction on a cancellation flow is #36's; accidental friction from an unconsidered flow is #42's — and the honest reviewer move is to surface it once, under the lens whose evidence actually fits, rather than twice.

### Tooling rules worth lifting

*(**Be honest about the coverage here:** interaction quality has the thinnest deterministic tooling of any category in the map. The mechanizable slice is real but small, and the pre-pass (`grounding-review-in-tool-output`) should report the rest as **judgment-only** rather than letting a clean lint run imply the flow was checked. That is not a weakness of the lens; it is the reason the lens exists.)*

- **`eslint-plugin-react` `jsx-no-leaked-render`** — flags `{count && <List/>}`-style conditional rendering that leaks a falsy value (a literal `0`) into the DOM instead of rendering nothing. Genuinely an **empty-state** defect caught mechanically: the branch that was meant to be the zero-data case renders a stray character.
- **`eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y` `no-autofocus`** — flags `autoFocus`, which moves focus without the user asking. Listed under a11y, but the reviewable failure is **controllability**: the system took the interaction lead.
- **`eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y` `tabindex-no-positive`** — a positive `tabindex` re-orders traversal away from the visual order, so the user's next step is not where they are looking. Ordering is #23's mechanics; the *surprise* is #42's.
- **`eslint-plugin-jsx-a11y` `no-access-key`** — access keys collide with the user's existing shortcuts. Same shape: the product overrides expectations the user brought with them.
- **`axe-core` `autocomplete-valid`** — an invalid `autocomplete` attribute means the browser cannot fill the field, so every user retypes what their browser already knows. An a11y rule with a usability payload for everyone.
- **Lighthouse `cumulative-layout-shift`** — content moving under the pointer between intention and click is Norman's slip, mechanized. Treat a CLS regression on an interactive surface as interaction evidence, not only performance evidence. Related Best-Practices audits (e.g. `errors-in-console`) are weaker signals `(verify — audit ids against your Lighthouse version)`.
- **Storybook stories as a state inventory** — not a rule but the most useful mechanical check available here: a component with a single `Default` story has no rendered evidence that its loading, empty, and error states were ever designed. Where a repo already uses Storybook, "which states have stories" is countable; `@storybook/addon-a11y` covers the #23 half. `(verify — addon name against your Storybook version)`
- **Type-level state completeness** — a discriminated union over `loading | empty | error | ready` makes the missing branch a **compiler** error rather than a review finding; a `data`-plus-two-booleans shape makes it invisible. This is the same illegal-states-unrepresentable move `reviewing-module-design` (#9/#10) already argues for, aimed at UI state. Where the stack allows it, prefer the type over the checklist — it is the only mechanization in this category that scales.

### Reviewable heuristics (skill-checklist seeds)

- ★ **Every state this code can reach has something designed for it.** An async read introduces at minimum **loading**, **empty / zero-data**, **error**, and **success**; a mutation adds **in-flight** and **failed**. A component that renders only the success path has shipped the others undesigned — the user sees a blank region, a spinner that never resolves, or a stray `0`. Enumerate the states from the code (the hook's flags, the union's variants, the promise's rejection path), not from the mockup, and name each one nothing handles. **This is a defect, not a preference:** the code produces the state whether or not anyone designed it.
- ★ **A destructive or irreversible action has a way back.** A new delete, archive, revoke, overwrite, cancel-subscription, or send flow needs **undo** (preferred, where the action can be deferred or reversed) or a confirmation that **names what will be lost** — "Delete 3 projects and 47 files?" and not "Are you sure?". A confirmation that names nothing is not error prevention; it is a click the user has been trained to dismiss. And check the *slip* case separately from the *mistake* case: a destructive control identical in size, colour, and position to its safe neighbour will be hit by accident no matter how good the confirmation copy is.
- **The system says what it is doing.** An operation slower than about a second reports that it started; one that can run long enough to lose the user's attention survives navigation away and back, and reports completion when they return. A fire-and-forget mutation with no success or failure feedback is the reviewable form of Norman's gulf of evaluation: the user cannot tell whether it worked, so they do it again.
- **Errors say what happened, why, and what to do next — and keep the user's work.** Three separate checks, and the third is the one reviews miss: an error path that clears a filled form, drops an upload, or resets a multi-step flow to step one is a **data-loss defect** wearing a copy problem's clothes. Wording quality routes to design (or to VII-D when it is built); losing the input does not.
- **The user sets the pace.** A modal with no dismiss, a step that auto-advances before the user has read it, a wizard with no way back, an interstitial that cannot be skipped on a return visit — each takes control the standards call the user's (ISO 9241-110 controllability; Nielsen's user control and freedom). Where the constraint is real — a legal acknowledgement, a payment step — say so; where it is incidental, it is a finding.
- **The change conforms to what this product already taught the user.** A second date picker, a dialog that confirms where the rest of the product undoes, a new empty-state that says something different from the four already shipped. Reviewable without design authority: does an equivalent already exist in this codebase, and does this one behave the same? The *decision* — adopt the existing pattern or change all of them — routes.
- **Recognition over recall.** A step that requires the user to remember or re-enter something the product already showed them or already knows: an ID to copy from a previous screen, a value the account record holds, a selection lost on navigation. Each is a checkable failure, not a matter of taste.
- **Route the taste, keep the gap.** Every finding this lens raises is one of two things, and it must say which. A **gap** — an unhandled state, an unrecoverable action, lost input, absent feedback — is a defect with ordinary severity and an engineering fix. A **judgment** — which pattern, which words, whether the flow should exist — is surfaced with its evidence and `route: design` or `route: product`, sets no engineering verdict, and is never argued to a conclusion inside the review. A lens that blocks a merge on a taste call has miscategorised its own finding.
- **Skip when there is no user-facing surface.** No UI, no CLI a human drives interactively, no user-facing flow — no findings. Say so in one line under coverage rather than manufacturing findings from a backend diff, and remember that a CLI *is* a user interface: prompts, progress, confirmation before destruction, and `--help` all fall here.

## #43 Product value & outcome instrumentation

> **Promoted v0.12 (map-gaps G24, VII-F); lens built this phase.** The second Cluster VII member, and the one that asks a question no other lens in the suite asks: **after this ships, how will anyone know whether it worked?** Every existing lens judges the change against its stated intent; this one judges whether the intent was stated in a form reality can contradict.
>
> **G1 single-owner & boundary.** #43 owns the link between a change and a **measurable user or business outcome**: is an outcome stated, is it measurable, is the instrumentation that would measure it in this diff, and is there a condition under which the change would be judged a failure. It does **not** own: whether a PR's *claims about the diff* are backed by evidence *in the diff* (**#24**'s claims-vs-evidence factor — "fixes X" needs a regression test; that is about the code, this is about production); operational metrics, SLOs, alerting, and debuggability (**#16** — an ops metric tells you the service is healthy, never that anyone got value; a change can be fully observable and completely unmeasured); the *schema* of an analytics event once it exists (**#40** — grain, compatibility, versioning, consumers; #43 owns whether the event exists at all and matches the plan); the decision record and its revisit trigger (**#29**/**#39**); feature-flag config hygiene and dead branches (**#26**/**#1**).
>
> **Detect-and-route, and the line is sharp.** *Whether this is the right outcome to pursue is not engineering's call* — that is `route: product`, surfaced with evidence, setting no verdict. What **is** reviewable, and is an ordinary defect: a change that claims a user benefit and ships no way to observe it; instrumentation promised in a follow-up that leaves the feature live and blind; an experiment with a success metric and no guardrail. The distinction is between *choosing* the goal and *being able to tell whether you reached it* — the second is engineering's, always.
>
> **The counterweight, stated up front.** This lens can do real damage if it becomes a demand for a metric on every commit. Refactors, bug fixes, dependency bumps, and internal work owe no outcome hypothesis; the trigger is a change that **claims a user or business benefit**, or that ships behind an experiment or flag. And instrumentation is code — it has cost, it can leak PII (**#27**), and "track everything" is its own failure mode. `checking-restraint` is the standing opposition, and it wins by default on anything that is not the stated outcome.

### Key references

- **Ronny Kohavi, Diane Tang & Ya Xu — *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments* (Cambridge University Press, 2020)** → mine: the discipline's most rigorous source, and three ideas the lens uses directly. The **OEC** (Overall Evaluation Criterion) — one agreed success metric decided *before* the experiment, not chosen afterward from whatever moved. **Guardrail metrics** — the things you are not allowed to break while chasing the win (latency, error rate, revenue, unsubscribes); an experiment with only a north-star metric can win it and lose the product. And **sample-ratio mismatch** — when the actual split diverges from the intended one, the experiment is broken and its result is not to be read, a check reviewable at the point the assignment code is written.
- **Eric Ries — *The Lean Startup* (2011)** → mine: **build-measure-learn** and the distinction between **vanity metrics** (cumulative totals that only go up — signups, page views, "users") and actionable ones (rates, cohorts, per-user behaviour). The reviewable form: a change whose stated success metric can only increase has not stated a falsifiable one.
- **Jeff Gothelf & Josh Seiden — *Lean UX*, and Seiden's *Outcomes Over Output* (2019)** → mine: the hypothesis statement as a **format**, which is what makes it checkable — *we believe [this change] will result in [this outcome]; we will know we are right when we see [this signal]*. And the framing the lens's name borrows: an **outcome** is a change in human behaviour, an **output** is a thing you shipped. Most PR descriptions describe outputs.
- **Marty Cagan — *Inspired*** → mine: the four risks a product idea must clear — **value** (will anyone want it), **usability** (can they use it), **feasibility**, and **viability** (does it work for the business). Useful as a routing map rather than a checklist: usability risk is #42's, feasibility is the rest of the suite's, and value/viability are what this lens surfaces and routes to product.
- **John Doerr — *Measure What Matters*** → mine: a key result is measurable and time-bound or it is not a key result. The lens's transferable move is the *shape* of a good statement (metric + direction + magnitude + date), not the OKR ceremony around it.
- **Marilyn Strathern's formulation of Goodhart's law (1997) — "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"** → mine: this lens's own counterweight, and the reason it must not simply demand a metric. A change that moves a proxy (clicks, session length, DAU) at plausible cost to the thing the proxy stood for should say so; the metric being *gameable by the product itself* is exactly where engagement optimisation turns into **#36**'s dark patterns, which is the cross-reference to make rather than the verdict to render.
- **OpenFeature (CNCF) — a vendor-neutral feature-flagging standard** — <https://openfeature.dev/> → mine: flags used for rollout and experiment are infrastructure with a lifecycle, so "who owns this flag and what ends it" is a reviewable property at the moment the flag is introduced, not a cleanup task discovered later. `(verify — specific SDK surface against your version)`
- **#40 `reviewing-data-transformations-and-contracts` — the boundary and the handoff.** An analytics event is a data contract: a producer emits it, dashboards and models consume it, and changing it breaks them. #40 owns that contract's schema, compatibility, and consumers. #43 owns the step before: **does the event exist, and does it match the plan the rest of the org reads.** A new event that no tracking plan knows about is invisible to every downstream dashboard however well-formed it is.
- **#24 `reviewing-pr-and-process-hygiene` — the adjacent discipline, deliberately not merged.** #24 already demands that a PR's claims be checkable against evidence *in the diff*. #43 is the production-side twin: a claim about the *world* ("this will reduce support tickets") cannot be settled by the diff, only instrumented. Keeping them separate is a G1 call — same intellectual move, different evidence surface, and merging them would put a production-measurability check inside a lens that never leaves the repository.

### Tooling rules worth lifting

*(Like #42, the mechanizable slice is small — and the two places it is real are **event-schema validation** and **flag lifecycle**. Everything about whether the outcome is the right one is judgment by construction.)*

- **Snowplow / Iglu schema validation** — events are validated against registered JSON schemas at collection, so a malformed or unregistered event fails loudly instead of landing in a table nobody notices is wrong. Where a repo has a schema registry for events, "is this new event registered" is a deterministic check, not a review opinion. `(verify — pipeline configuration against your setup)`
- **Tracking-plan governance tooling** — Avo, Amplitude's data-governance surface, and Segment Protocols all express a **tracking plan** as a versioned artifact that emitted events are validated against; several generate typed client code from it, which turns "the event name is a typo" into a compile error. Name the artifact if the repo has one, and treat divergence from it exactly as `auditing-enforcement-and-meta-artifacts` (#30) treats monitoring-config drift. `(verify — product capabilities against current vendor docs)`
- **Feature-flag reference scanning** — LaunchDarkly's `ld-find-code-refs` and Unleash's stale-flag reporting both connect flags in the management system to their references in code, which is what makes "this flag outlived its experiment" a report rather than a memory. `(verify — tool names and capabilities against current docs)`
- **Experiment platforms as the guardrail surface** — GrowthBook (open source), Statsig, and similar platforms model the experiment as a configured object with metrics attached, so *which* metrics an experiment declares — and whether any of them are guardrails rather than success metrics — is inspectable config, not prose. `(verify)`
- **What is deliberately not here.** There is no linter for "this hypothesis is falsifiable," and there should not be one: the check is whether a human wrote down a losing condition, which is a reading task. Do not let the presence of a tracking-plan validator imply the outcome question was answered — report it as judgment-only coverage.

### Reviewable heuristics (skill-checklist seeds)

- ★ **A change that claims a user or business benefit says what it expects to move, and how anyone would see it.** The check is on the *claim*, not on every commit: "adds an export button" owes nothing; "reduces the support load from manual exports" owes a way to observe support load. A description that names only what was built cannot be wrong about anything, which is the problem. Where the claim is real and the observation is absent, that is the finding — and the fix is usually one event, not a measurement programme.
- ★ **The instrumentation that would prove the change ships in the same diff.** Deferred instrumentation is the characteristic failure of this category: the feature goes live, the follow-up is deprioritised because the feature already works, and six months later nobody can say whether it helped. If the outcome metric depends on an event, the event is in this change; if it depends on a dashboard, the change says which one and whether it exists. "We'll add tracking later" is a finding, not a plan.
- **Outcome metrics are not operational metrics.** A new request counter, latency histogram, or error-rate alert tells you the feature *runs*; none of them tells you anyone was better off. Both may be needed and neither substitutes — when a change ships thorough #16 instrumentation and no outcome signal, say exactly that rather than accepting the ops metrics as coverage.
- **The hypothesis has a losing condition.** A stated expectation that cannot fail is not a hypothesis: it needs a metric, a direction, a rough magnitude, and a horizon — and a decision attached to the failing branch. **"We will keep it regardless" is a legitimate answer** and should be recorded as one; the defect is leaving the question unasked, so that the feature stays by default because nobody set a bar it could miss.
- **An experiment declares its guardrails, not only its win condition.** Kohavi's point, and the most commonly skipped one: name the metrics that must *not* degrade — latency, error rate, conversion elsewhere in the funnel, unsubscribes, revenue — before the rollout, not after someone notices. An experiment that can only be evaluated on the metric it was designed to move is one that cannot detect the damage it does.
- **Check the assignment, not just the analysis.** Where a diff contains randomisation or bucketing code, the reviewable properties are concrete: is assignment stable for a given user across sessions, is the unit of assignment the same as the unit of analysis, is the exposure logged at the point the user actually sees the variant rather than where the flag is read, and would a sample-ratio mismatch be visible to anyone. These are code properties, checkable now, and each one silently invalidates the result later.
- **New events match the plan the rest of the organisation reads.** Name, casing, and required properties consistent with the existing event taxonomy; registered in the tracking plan or schema registry where one exists; and no PII in properties (**#27** owns that verdict — surface and route it). An event that diverges from the plan is not wrong, it is *invisible*: every downstream dashboard filters it out. Schema compatibility and consumers are **#40**'s.
- **A flag introduced for a rollout or experiment has an owner and an end condition.** What removes it, and when — the experiment concluding, the rollout completing, a date. Flags without one become permanent untested branches and quietly double the states the system can be in; the lifecycle detail is **#26**'s and the dead branch is **#1**'s, but the moment to ask is when the flag is added.
- **Watch for the proxy that has become the target.** When the stated metric is a proxy (clicks, time on page, session count, DAU) and the change plausibly moves it at the expense of what the proxy stood for, say so and route it. This is Goodhart, and it is the seam where outcome optimisation becomes **#36**'s manipulative design — surface the tension, name both readings, and let product decide. Never adjudicate it inside an engineering review.
- **Route the goal, keep the measurability.** Whether the outcome is worth pursuing, which metric best represents it, and what magnitude counts as success are product judgments: surfaced with evidence, `route: product`, no engineering verdict. Whether a stated outcome can be observed at all, and whether the code that would observe it is present, is an engineering fact and an ordinary defect. Never state a preference about the product's direction as though it were the second kind.
- **Skip when the change makes no user-facing or business claim.** Refactors, bug fixes, dependency bumps, build and infra work, and internal tooling owe no outcome hypothesis — demanding one is the failure mode that would make this lens a nuisance, and it is more likely than the failure it guards against. Say the lens did not apply, in one line, and stop.
